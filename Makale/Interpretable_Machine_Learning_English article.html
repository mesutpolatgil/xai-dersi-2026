Interpretable Machine Learning: Why Prediction Is Not Enough

Abstract

A machine-learning model may achieve excellent accuracy and still rely on patterns that are misleading or unsuitable for real decisions. This article reviews how interpretable machine learning and explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) help us examine those patterns. It begins with the problem of shortcut learning, then discusses the difference between explaining a model as a whole and explaining one prediction. The review considers permutation importance, partial dependence, LIME, and SHAP, alongside models designed to be understandable from the outset. A hypothetical customer-churn case illustrates both the value and the limits of feature explanations. The article argues that explanations should be assessed for faithfulness, stability, practical usefulness, and robustness. Above all, an explanation tells us something about a model's behaviour; it does not, on its own, prove that a decision is causal, fair, or safe.

Scope and contribution

This article is a narrative review of several influential ideas and methods in XAI. Rather than claiming to cover every study, it brings together foundational research, explains the main mathematical ideas, and discusses how explanations can be evaluated in practice. No new experiment or systematic literature search is reported.

The question running through the review is straightforward: when does an explanation genuinely help us judge whether a model should be trusted for a particular decision?

1. Introduction: the limits of prediction

A classifier can perform impressively on a test dataset without learning the relationship we hoped it would learn. It may instead pick up a coincidence, an artefact of data collection, or a variable that would be inappropriate to use in practice. Accuracy tells us how often predictions agree with recorded labels under a given test. It does not tell us whether the model's reasoning will remain useful when circumstances change. This matters particularly in healthcare, lending, recruitment, and public services, where a mistaken prediction can affect people's lives (Molnar, 2022; Rudin, 2019).

Interpretability is not one technique or one score. It is a set of ways to investigate what a model uses to produce its outputs. Before choosing an explanation method, we need to ask who will read the explanation, what they need to decide, and how closely the explanation reflects the original model. This review focuses on those practical questions rather than treating an attractive explanation as evidence of reliability.

Prediction, generalization, and deployment

Test results depend on how the data were collected and divided. If the same misleading pattern appears in both the training and test sets, a model can look reliable even though it would struggle elsewhere. Testing on later time periods, different hospitals, or independently collected samples can reveal weaknesses that a random train–test split might miss.

Interpretability is only part of responsible deployment. Teams also need to check data quality, probability calibration, decision thresholds, performance over time, and routes for reviewing or challenging harmful outputs. Explanations help identify questions worth investigating, but they cannot replace these checks.

Illustrative distinction

Consider a model that predicts a patient's risk partly from the hospital where the patient was treated. The hospital identifier may correlate with the outcome in historical records, yet it does not necessarily explain the patient's medical condition. An XAI method could reveal this dependence. Deciding whether it is clinically acceptable would still require medical expertise and independent validation.

2. The Clever Hans problem and shortcut learning

Clever Hans was a horse that seemed able to solve arithmetic problems. In fact, he was responding to subtle signals from people who knew the answers. The lesson for machine learning is striking: a system can appear to solve the intended problem while using a completely different clue. A wolf classifier, for example, might learn to associate snow with wolves rather than identify the animal itself. Such shortcuts can disappear when the model is used in a new setting (Molnar, 2022).

An explanation may draw attention to a suspicious feature, but that is the beginning of an investigation, not the end. To understand whether a model has learned a shortcut, we should also inspect the data, test relevant subgroups, change suspect inputs where appropriate, and evaluate the model on new or deliberately shifted samples. A highlighted image region, for example, is a useful clue but not proof of a causal mechanism.

Sources of shortcuts

Shortcuts can enter a datasets in many ways. Information about the answer may leak into the inputs; image backgrounds may correlate with labels; recording devices may leave distinctive traces; or a seemingly harmless variable may stand in for a sensitive characteristic. Flexible models can exploit these signals because they help predict the labels seen during training.

A practical audit might compare explanations across datasets, remove a suspected shortcut where this is feasible, and test performance after the change. If an attribution plot looks different, that is worth examining. It does not automatically mean the underlying problem has been solved.

Audit questions

Useful questions include: Would this feature be available when the model is actually used? Is it connected to the phenomenon we want to predict? Could it be a proxy for an inappropriate characteristic? Does the relationship persist in data collected elsewhere? Asking these questions helps move from a plausible story toward a defensible assessment.

3. Conceptual foundations

3.1 Global versus local explanation

Global explanations describe patterns in a model's behaviour across many cases. They can show which inputs matter overall or where a model responds non-linearly. Local explanations address a narrower question: why did the model produce this output for this person or observation? A local explanation may fit one case well without describing the model elsewhere. Similarly, a global average can hide behaviour affecting a small but important group (Molnar, 2022; Ribeiro et al., 2016).

3.2 Intrinsic versus post-hoc interpretability

Some models are built so that people can inspect their decision logic directly. Examples include short rule lists, small decision trees, and sparse linear models. Other models are examined after training through post-hoc methods that approximate or summarize their behaviour. The distinction is useful, but not absolute. A linear model with hundreds of correlated variables may be hard to understand, while a carefully designed additive model may remain readable despite capturing nonlinear effects (Rudin, 2019).

3.3 Explanation is not causation

Knowing which feature influenced a prediction is different from knowing what would happen if we changed that feature in the real world. For example, a churn model might rely heavily on recorded billing complaints. That does not prove that clearing a complaint will prevent the customer from leaving. Questions about interventions require domain knowledge and, ideally, experiments or sound causal analysis.

Taxonomy of interpretability

Dimension Question answered Typical technique
Global What does the model generally rely on? Permutation importance, PDP
Local Why this particular output? LIME, SHAP
Intrinsic Can the decision structure itself be inspected? Sparse score, short tree
Post-hoc Can an existing fitted model be analyzed? Surrogate, attribution

These categories overlap. A simple linear model can be explained at both the global and individual levels, and a post-hoc technique may be used for either purpose. Interpretability also depends on the reader: a rule set that seems clear to a data scientist may still be difficult for a patient to understand.

4. Core explainability methods

4.1 Permutation importance and partial dependence

Permutation importance asks how much a model's performance worsens when the values of one feature are shuffled. For a fitted model f, evaluation dataset D, and loss L, we write I_j = L(f, D with feature j permuted) − L(f, D). A larger positive value suggests that the model depends more heavily on that feature for the selected test and loss. Partial dependence takes a different approach: PD_j(z) = E_X−j[f(z, X−j)] summarizes the model's average output when feature j is set to z. Neither calculation, by itself, measures a causal effect (Molnar, 2022).

4.2 Local interpretable model-agnostic explanations (LIME)

LIME explains one prediction by fitting a simpler model near the observation of interest. Its objective can be written g* = arg min_g { L(f, g, π_x) + Ω(g) }. Here, π_x gives greater weight to nearby observations, while Ω(g) discourages an overly complicated explanation. The appeal is that a short local rule or linear approximation is easier to discuss than a complex predictor. The weakness is that the explanation may become misleading if the sampled neighbourhood is unrealistic or the original model behaves irregularly nearby (Ribeiro et al., 2016).

4.3 SHAP and additive attribution

SHAP approaches explanations through cooperative game theory. It distributes the difference between a model's output and a reference value across the input features. A common additive expression is f(x) ≈ φ₀ + Σ_j φ_j, where φ₀ is the baseline and φ_j is the contribution assigned to feature j. The precise meaning of those contributions depends on how the reference population and feature combinations are defined. They may also be expressed in log-odds rather than probability, a distinction that matters when presenting results (Lundberg & Lee, 2017).

Assumptions and practical interpretation

Permutation importance is sensitive to the dataset and loss function used for evaluation. It can also be difficult to interpret when features are strongly correlated. Shuffling one variable may create combinations that rarely occur in reality, while shared predictive information can make either variable seem less important than expected. Grouped or conditional analyses may help.

Partial dependence plots average the model's response as a chosen feature changes. They are useful for spotting broad trends, but the averaging process can hide differences between individuals. It may also create feature combinations that are implausible in the real population. Individual conditional expectation plots offer a useful companion view.

SHAP and LIME: interpret carefully

Both SHAP and LIME require careful choices. SHAP depends on the background distribution, the output scale, and how missing features are represented. LIME depends on the observations generated around the case and how the surrogate is fitted. Neither method automatically identifies causes or tells someone which action will improve an outcome.

A sensible check is to compare a local surrogate's predictions with those of the original model on plausible nearby observations. When results depend strongly on a random seed or a chosen baseline, that sensitivity should be reported rather than hidden.

5. Worked illustration: customer churn

Imagine a subscription service whose churn model assigns one customer a cancellation probability of 0.72, compared with a reference value of 0.30. The model might respond to repeated billing disputes, a recent price increase, and the customer's long history with the service. This is a constructed teaching example: no model was trained and no SHAP values were computed for it.

The prediction tells the company whom it may need to pay attention to. The explanation suggests which recorded factors influenced that prediction. Neither tells the company which intervention will work. A discount, a billing correction, or simply fewer marketing messages could have very different effects, and those effects need to be tested.

Illustrative attribution breakdown

Component Illustrative contribution to probability
Reference prediction 0.30
Repeated billing disputes +0.23
Recent price increase +0.14
Long tenure −0.05
Other model factors +0.10
Illustrative prediction 0.72

The numbers in the table are illustrative contributions chosen to make the additive calculation easy to follow. They should not be read as results from a fitted model. In an actual SHAP analysis, the explanation may be calculated on a log-odds scale rather than directly in probabilities.

From explanation to intervention

Suppose billing disputes are the strongest positive contribution. The model may be using that history as a warning signal. Whether resolving a dispute would reduce churn is a different question. An A/B test or a well-designed observational study would provide stronger evidence about the intervention.

The case highlights three questions that are easy to confuse: Who is likely to leave? Which inputs influenced the model's prediction? And what action would actually help retain the customer? XAI is most directly concerned with the second question; the third requires evidence about real-world effects.

6. How should explanations be evaluated?

An explanation should not be judged only by whether its chart is attractive or easy to read. Evaluation should ask whether it reflects the original model, whether it changes unpredictably after small input changes, whether it remains useful on new data, and whether people can make better decisions with it.

These goals can pull in different directions. A short explanation may be clear but leave out an important interaction. A detailed explanation may follow the mathematics closely yet overwhelm its audience. The right balance depends on the decision and on the people who need to use the explanation.

Criterion Practical evaluation
Fidelity Compare local surrogate outputs with the original model
Stability Repeat explanations after small, plausible perturbations
Robustness Check sensitivity under distribution shift
Usefulness Test whether intended users make better decisions
Fairness Audit subgroup patterns and proxy dependence
Reproducibility Record data, model, seed, baseline and software version

Human-centered evaluation

A chart can create confidence without improving understanding. Human-centred evaluations should therefore test whether clinicians, analysts, or affected individuals can spot model errors, recognize uncertainty, and make better judgments when explanations are available. Feeling more confident is not the same as being better informed.

Different uses call for different tests. A debugging explanation should help identify faults. An explanation used in an appeal should make the reasons for a decision understandable. An audit requires traceable evidence about how the system behaves across cases and groups.

7. Interpretable models versus black-box explanations

Rudin (2019) argues that, especially when decisions have serious consequences, we should first consider models whose reasoning can be inspected directly. This challenges the common assumption that more accurate systems must always be harder to understand. Whether that trade-off exists depends on the problem and the data. Complex models may still be justified, but they need stronger evidence than a convincing post-hoc explanation alone.

A fair comparison should evaluate transparent and complex models using the same data splits and the same relevant criteria, including accuracy, calibration, robustness, and fairness. If a complex model offers a meaningful improvement, that benefit must be weighed against the extra work needed to examine and govern it. If the results are similar, a simpler model may be easier to justify.

A balanced selection principle

It makes sense to begin with an understandable baseline rather than assuming a black-box model is necessary. A short rule list or additive model may already perform well enough for the task, while offering a clearer path for review and accountability.

Some tasks genuinely benefit from more complex models. In those cases, post-hoc explanations can be valuable, but they should sit alongside independent validation, stress testing, documentation, and human oversight. They are not a certificate of safety.

High-stakes decisions

The seriousness of a decision depends on more than model complexity. We should consider how harmful an error could be, whether it can be reversed, whether a person can review it, and whether the affected individual has a meaningful way to challenge the result.

8. A practical XAI workflow

A useful XAI workflow starts with the decision, not the software library. First identify who will be affected and what the model is supposed to support. Then examine where the data came from, check for leakage and gaps, establish an interpretable baseline, and evaluate candidate models. Only after that should the team choose explanation methods and test whether the resulting explanations are faithful and stable.

When an explanation is reported, readers should be able to tell which model and dataset were used, what the output represents, what reference population was selected, and which limitations apply. Observations about model behaviour must be kept separate from claims about causes, fairness, or the effectiveness of an intervention.

Recommended reporting record

A useful record includes the decision context, dataset origin and exclusions, model version, validation procedure, output scale, explanation settings, reference population, tests of fidelity and stability, subgroup checks, and a clear account of what the explanation cannot establish.

9. Discussion, limitations, and future directions

The challenge in XAI is not simply to make complex models easier to describe. It is to make explanations informative without making them sound more certain than they are. Post-hoc methods can reveal useful patterns, yet their conclusions may change with the baseline or the data. Transparent models are easier to inspect, but they can still be biased, poorly calibrated, or unsuitable for the task.

This article draws on selected foundational studies rather than attempting an exhaustive review. It does not compare methods experimentally or claim that one technique performs best in every setting. Future studies should examine explanations under changing data conditions and test whether they actually help different users make better decisions.

Particularly valuable directions include explanation stability under distribution shift, causal testing of proposed interventions, studies involving real decision-makers, and more reliable ways to explain foundation models. The aim should be evidence that explanations improve understanding and decisions, not merely better-looking visualizations.

10. Conclusion

Accurate prediction matters, but it is not the whole story. Clever Hans reminds us that a model can succeed for the wrong reason. Global and local explanations help us inspect different parts of its behaviour, while inherently interpretable models offer a way to make the decision structure visible from the start. Still, an explanation is not verification: feature importance is not causation, a polished plot is not proof of faithfulness, and transparency alone does not guarantee fairness. Responsible use requires careful model choice, independent testing, and people who remain accountable for consequential decisions.
